Three ways to draw a pine tree

One picture of a pine tree on a small hill, run through three drawing routes in the background pipeline. Each route makes a different kind of image, and each one earns that look with a different set of algorithms. This page follows the actual code paths, step by step, with the real intermediate images and the real numbers from the run.

0. The source picture

Two bitmaps, same scene. The left one is flat colour only: no outlines, hard edges. The right one adds a 3 px ink outline to every object, and that is what a line-art source looks like. The flat route wants the first, the comic and sketch routes want the second.

Composition matters more than the code. Every route was run on a version of this scene that filled the frame edge to edge, and the ink route failed its own coverage gate on it. The page SVG clips at the canvas edge, so an outline drawn on the edge loses half its line and the gate counts it as missing ink. A frame around the whole page is the same problem from another side: it is ink in every border cell. Line art on paper has margins, so the source does too now, and the ground is a closed mound instead of a full-width strip.

1. Flat: trace the regions

flat.py bg — the region engine.

This route treats the picture as a map of solid colour patches. Find the patches, pull each outline slightly inward so neighbours do not fight, then redraw each patch with a wobbly hand-drawn brush. Nothing is inferred: what you see is what the colours say.

Step 1 — mark the extremes

Pure white and pure black are special, because the tracer normally treats them as paper and as ink it should discard. They get swapped for two loud sentinels first (#FF00B4 for white, #00C85A for black) so they survive tracing as real content, then get painted back. The picture now has a magenta sky, which is alarming and temporary.

Step 2 — trace closed regions

Colours are grouped, connected components are found, and each component's boundary is traced into a closed polygon. The result is a hard partition of the page: every pixel belongs to exactly one region, and neighbouring regions share an edge.

Region count from the run: 8 including the paper, so 7 drawn shapes (mound, three pine tiers, trunk, sun, bush).

Why the outline has to move. A traced page is a partition, so every closed outline sits centred on the shared edge and spreads half its brush width outward, over whatever was drawn before it. Draw the rocks after the figure and the figure loses its legs. So each compact outline is inset by size/2 - 2.5 px: it lands inside its own region, leaving a 2.5 px overlap that the neighbour's outline covers. Slivers that do not survive the inset are antialias fringe and get dropped. Limbs are drawn as strokes as wide as the part, so they are not inset.

2. Comic: keep the ink, flatten the rest

flat.py bg --comic — the same region engine with an ink recovery pass.

This route is for sources that already have line work: a sketch, a cartoon with black outlines. The trick is to separate the drawing into two layers, a colour layer and an ink layer, clean the colour layer up as if it were flat art, then put the ink back on top as fresh hand strokes.

Step 1 — take the ink out

Ink is any pixel that is much darker than the local neighbourhood (a 9 px median), grown by a pixel for the antialiased edge. Then the neighbouring colours grow over the vacated ink, so an outline between tree and sky becomes tree or sky rather than a gap.

A dark mass is not a stroke. A navy suit, a black silhouette, a dark tree tier: if ink removal ate those, the picture would lose whole objects. So before removing anything, the code computes the Euclidean distance transform of the dark mask and keeps every blob whose half-width reaches 7 px — that is a fill, not a line — growing each one back to its own island. On this scene nothing was protected: the three tiers are separate wide shapes, and their black outlines are thin enough to be removed and redrawn as 18 strokes.

Step 2 — cut the palette, kill the hatch

The clean colour layer is quantized to 24 colours with an octree, then a 7 px mode filter runs over the palette indices. The mode filter is what erases pencil hatching and stray specks: each pixel takes the most common palette index in its neighbourhood, so a lone dark scratch in a green tier disappears.

Step 3 — thin the ink and redraw it

The ink mask is thinned to a one-pixel skeleton with Zhang–Suen, L-shaped corner spurs are pruned, and the skeleton is walked as chains. Each chain becomes a brush stroke whose width comes from the original blob's thickness, and whose colour is sampled from the source pixels underneath it.

Zhang–Suen thinning. Repeatedly delete boundary pixels that have 2–6 neighbours and exactly one 0→1 transition around their 8-neighbour ring, alternating two deletion rules so the line stays connected and does not drift sideways. What survives is the centre line of the stroke, one pixel wide, which is what a brush can follow.

3. Sketch: distance transform the ink

ink.py sketch — the ink engine.

This is the opposite approach. It does not start from colour regions at all; it starts from the ink and asks a geometric question at every ink pixel: how wide is the line here? Thin lines become strokes. Wide ink becomes a filled region. That one measurement replaces the whole colour-segmentation stage.

Step 1 — find the ink

Paper colour is read from the top rows. Ink is a pixel whose strongest channel has dropped from the paper colour, or which is locally darker than its surroundings (hysteresis: a high threshold seeds, a low threshold connects). Faded stroke ends are then walked outward along their own tangent so marker ramps are not lost. On this picture the mask comes out identical to the comic route's ink layer: both are the same "darker than the neighbourhood" test, expressed with a different metric.

Step 2 — split by width

For every ink pixel, compute the distance to the nearest non-ink pixel. That distance is the local half-width of the ink. A stroke has a small radius; a filled blob has a large one. Split at r ≥ 7.0.

Euclidean distance transform. One pass computes, for every ink pixel, the distance to the closest paper pixel. On a 3 px outline the maximum is ~1.5, so the whole line is "stroke". On a filled pupil or a solid tier the interior climbs past 7, and that core is grown back by 7 - 0.5 px, clipped to its own connected ink island, to recover the true region boundary without a leftover donut of stroke around it.

Each stroke chain gets a width from its own radius, so a thick contour draws thick and a fine detail draws fine, without any per-line settings.

Step 3 — wash the enclosed paper

With outlines only, the picture is blank paper with lines on it. --auto-tint finds the closed pockets the ink encloses, samples the source colour inside each, and paints a translucent marker wash over it, angled per pocket so the strokes look hand laid.

Wash opacity is deliberately low. The mark is a hint of colour, not a fill: the drawing should still read as ink on paper, which is why the palette of the sketch result is softer and lighter than the flat result even though the source colours are the same.

5. Numbers

Measured on this scene, 640×480 page, rendered at 2× (1280×960), three runs each.

The sketch route is about 4× slower, and the time is not in the tracing: with --auto-tint removed it runs in 1.5 s, so nearly 5 s of the 6.2 is the wash pass — finding enclosed pockets, sampling each one, and stamping hundreds of translucent marker strokes at the right per-pocket angle. The coverage gate adds nothing measurable (6.1 s with --no-budget-check), but it is the thing that caught this scene's composition problems.
